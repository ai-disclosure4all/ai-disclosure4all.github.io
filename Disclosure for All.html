Disclosure for All AI

Accessible and culturally grounded AI provenance

AI-generated and AI-edited media travel across platforms and communities. The labels, icons, and Content Credentials meant to disclose how that media was made are often hard to find, inaccessible, or read differently across cultures. This in-person workshop brings together accessibility, HCI, media, security, and standards communities to design disclosure that works for everyone.

Themes

Four themes, four research questions

  1. Information needs and provenance

    Provenance standards can record how content was created or modified. We know less about which parts of that history people need in different contexts, and how to present it without it being read as a guarantee of truth.

    RQ1What information about AI involvement and content history do people need to understand and act on provenance in different contexts, and what can a provenance record meaningfully support?

  2. Perceivability across abilities and modalities

    AI disclosures are designed mainly around visual cues. Do they communicate equivalent information through visual, auditory, and haptic access, and across cultures?

    RQ2How can disclosures communicate equivalent and actionable information across access modes, languages, and platform interfaces?

  3. Culture, language, and community

    Security warnings have historically been designed for Western users, and many internet users live outside the contexts current AI labels were made for. Designing with Global South communities, low-resource language speakers, and sign language users can surface different understandings of authorship, editing, evidence, and uncertainty.

    RQ3How do cultural norms, community histories, language, and the stakes of an interaction shape how people interpret AI involvement, authorship, editing, evidence, and uncertainty?

  4. Methods, standards, and governance

    HCI research can supply the design and evaluation evidence that standards and regulations need, so accessibility and cultural context are built in from the start instead of added after technical standards are set.

    RQ4What strategies are needed for engaging diverse communities in evaluating the effectiveness of AI disclosure, and informing standards?

Call for participation

Submit a position paper

What to submit

A 2–4 page position paper or statement of interest describing relevant research, practice, experience, a concrete case, or an open question. Accessible alternative formats are welcome.

Example topics

  • Whether AI disclosures are understandable with screen readers and other assistive technologies
  • How provenance cues translate across languages, sign languages, and cultural contexts
  • How disclosure needs vary by platform, modality, and the stakes of an interaction
  • What users need from C2PA and Content Credentials compared with what they record
  • Methods for co-designing and evaluating disclosure with diverse communities

Who should apply

HCI researchers Accessibility practitioners Creators Journalists Platform specialists Standards specialists Policymakers

We especially welcome people with disabilities and contributors from the Global South and low-resource language communities.

Review and publication

Submissions are reviewed for relevance and potential to contribute to discussion. We will select participants across disciplines, regions, career stages, and perspectives. With author consent, accepted contributions will be shared with participants two weeks before the workshop and published on the workshop website (for example, linked to arXiv), alongside an accessible synthesis of outcomes.

At least one author of each accepted submission should attend in person.
